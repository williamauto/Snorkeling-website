Fort Lauderdale Area

Broward County's shore-entry reef line, from Lauderdale-by-the-Sea down to Hollywood.

Lauderdale-by-the-Sea

A compact beach town built around a shore-entry reef just 100 yards out, no boat needed for most of it.

Nearly everything here is walk-in from the beach.→

Fort Lauderdale Beach

A mix of one shore reef and several boat-access ledges running parallel to the beach.

Mostly boat access; one shore option.→

Pompano Beach

Home to one of Broward's best-known historic wrecks, the SS Copenhagen.

Boat access only.→

Dania Beach

A quieter stretch with one named reef near the Dania Pier.

Boat access only.→

Hollywood

Shore snorkeling at North Beach Park plus the offshore 1000 Mermaids art reef.

Mixed access: one shore spot, one boat-only spot.→
Miami

South Beach, Key Biscayne, and Biscayne National Park: Miami-Dade's mix of shore reefs and protected park water.

South Beach

Boat-access natural reef and ledges just south of Government Cut.

Boat access only; there is no shore snorkeling reef in South Beach proper.→

Key Biscayne

Protected park water, home to Miami's most popular shallow reef plus two offbeat boat trips.

Tour or private-boat access; no walk-in reef.→

Biscayne National Park

About 95 percent of the park is underwater, so the only way to see the reef is by boat, tour, or the park concessionaire.

Boat or tour required; no shore reef access.→
Florida Keys

Key Largo and Islamorada, home to the start of the Great Florida Reef and John Pennekamp Coral Reef State Park.

Key Largo

John Pennekamp Coral Reef State Park and the offshore reefs that made Key Largo the dive capital of the Keys.

One shore option inside the state park; the famous offshore reefs are boat or tour access.→

Islamorada

A string of named patch reefs and a Sanctuary Preservation Area, almost all boat access.

Boat access only for the named reefs; Founder's Park has limited shore swimming.→
